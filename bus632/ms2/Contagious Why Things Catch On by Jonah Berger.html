Contagious: Why Things Catch On by Jonah Berger

Narrative Summary of Chapters 1 & 2 – “Social Currency” and “Triggers”
For BUS 632: The Content Composer

Chapter 1: Social Currency — Why People Talk (and What They Gain From It)

Imagine you’re at a dinner party. You drop a clever stat: “You know that secret bar in NYC that’s hidden behind a phone booth?”
The room leans in. You just earned a few invisible points of social status — not by bragging, but by sharing something exclusive.

That’s social currency, and according to Jonah Berger, it’s one of the most powerful engines behind viral content. In this chapter, Berger makes a clear case: People share things not just to inform, but to impress. The content we pass along becomes a subtle signal of who we are — our taste, intelligence, humor, values, and even insider knowledge.

Why This Matters for Storytelling:

Your content isn’t just competing for attention — it’s competing for identity value. If it doesn’t make someone look good, feel clever, or seem “in the know,” they’re less likely to share it.

Berger’s Core Lessons on Social Currency:

  • We share things that reflect who we are or want to be.
    Just like we choose outfits to reflect our style, we “wear” our digital shares — posts, articles, memes — to say something about ourselves.

  • Remarkability drives conversation.
    People talk about things that are surprising, novel, clever, or entertaining. Berger uses the concept of “inner remarkability” — the hidden gem, the surprising fact, the product that defies expectation. These things make us pause and say, “Wait — really?”

  • Secrets and exclusivity are powerful.
    Things that feel insider or hard to access are especially likely to be shared. The less available something feels, the more valuable it becomes. The secret menu at In-N-Out. The velvet rope at Please Don’t Tell. The “invite-only” label. If your brand can create a sense of exclusivity or discovery, you’ve tapped into a core part of what makes things spread.

  • Game mechanics (status + competition) increase visibility.
    Platforms that offer points, badges, or “levels” — think Yelp Elite or Reddit Karma — give users status symbols to chase and share. Smart brands use this psychology to reward participation and encourage community storytelling.

Application to Digital Content Creation:

  • Tell stories that highlight unique facts or surprising results.

  • Create campaigns that reward the audience for engaging and sharing (early access, exclusives, shoutouts).

  • Use brand voice and visuals that make people feel like they’re part of something “cool” — not just being sold to.

Chapter 2: Triggers — How to Stay Top-of-Mind in a Distracted World

Now shift scenes. It’s Friday. Someone posts that awful yet iconic Rebecca Black music video. You groan… and then you share it.
Why? Not because it’s remarkable — but because it’s Friday, and that song has become wired to the day in your brain.

This is Berger’s next major insight: what’s top-of-mind is more likely to be tip-of-tongue. And what becomes top-of-mind is often dictated by triggers — the cues embedded in our daily routines, environments, or even language that remind us of a product, idea, or message.

 Why This Matters for Storytelling:

A story is only as powerful as its timing. The best stories don’t just grab attention — they return to people’s thoughts again and again, often when we least expect it. When your content is attached to a frequently encountered cue, it increases your chances of being remembered and shared.

Berger’s Core Lessons on Triggers:

  • Create associations between your brand and frequent, familiar things.
    It could be a time of day, a color, a season, a pop culture event, a habit. When you create content that links your brand to something people already experience regularly, you ride the wave of their subconscious.

  • More frequent triggers = more consistent sharing.
    You don’t need to go viral overnight. You need to stay visible, steadily. A snack that’s linked to “afternoon slump” might get shared every day at 3 p.m. That’s longevity — not flash-in-the-pan buzz.

  • Context drives memory.
    People remember content when it’s tied to their daily lives. That’s why Kit Kat sales soared when ads paired it with coffee breaks: "Have a break, have a Kit Kat." The campaign created a habitual trigger — coffee → Kit Kat.

  • Emotional intensity fades; triggers endure.
    Not every piece of content can be hilarious or shocking. But if you connect it to a repeating behavior or situation, it’ll keep resurfacing — and that’s how it spreads.

Application to Digital Content Creation:

  • Anchor your content to daily habits or weekly rhythms (e.g., “Monday Motivation,” “Wellness Wednesday”).

  • Pair brand messages with specific moments (e.g., fitness content in the morning, snack brands in the late afternoon).

  • Use language, visuals, or music that already lives in your audience’s environment — to make your brand feel familiar.
