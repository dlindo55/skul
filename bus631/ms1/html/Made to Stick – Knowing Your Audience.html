Introduction to Made to Stick

By Chip Heath & Dan Heath
Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die

About the Book

Made to Stick is a bestselling book on communication, storytelling, and idea design, written by brothers Chip Heath, a professor at Stanford Graduate School of Business, and Dan Heath, a senior fellow at Duke University’s CASE Center. Drawing from decades of research in cognitive psychology, behavioral economics, and organizational communication, the Heath brothers explore what makes certain messages “stick”—that is, why some ideas are understood, remembered, and acted upon while others are forgotten almost instantly.

First published in 2007, Made to Stick remains one of the most influential books on communication strategy across business, marketing, nonprofit, and educational sectors.

Why It Matters for Business Communicators

In leadership and organizational communication, having the right message isn’t enough. Your message needs to cut through the noise, engage your audience, and drive action—whether you’re writing a memo, pitching a product, giving a keynote, or explaining a complex change initiative.

The Heath brothers argue that "sticky" ideas share six key traits, summarized in the SUCCESS framework:

  • Simplicity

  • Unexpectedness

  • Concreteness

  • Credibility

  • Emotion

  • Stories

Throughout the book, they use vivid real-world examples—from urban legends to business case studies—to show how to transform flat, forgettable messages into ideas that stick.

Why Audience Matters in Sticky Messages

The Heath brothers argue that no matter how important, accurate, or strategic your message is, it won’t stick unless it is designed with the audience’s mindset in mind. This includes their prior knowledge, values, cognitive biases, emotional triggers, and attention span.

They introduce a six-part SUCCESS framework for “sticky” ideas, but several of these elements directly hinge on understanding your audience:

  • Simplicity

  • Unexpectedness

  • Concreteness

  • (plus Stories, Emotion, and Credibility)

We’ll focus here on the elements most critical to decoding and tailoring messages for your audience.

Core Concepts for Audience-Centered Communication

1. Simplicity: Find the Core—Then Say It Clearly

Key Insight:
People can’t remember or act on your message if they don’t understand it first. Knowing your audience means distilling what’s most relevant to them, not just what’s important to you.

Strategies:

  • Identify the single most important idea for the audience—what’s the core of your message?

  • Use analogies or comparisons to explain unfamiliar ideas (e.g., “We’re building the Spotify of fitness.”)

  • Translate complexity into audience-specific language—don’t overwhelm with unnecessary data.

Audience Application:

  • With internal staff, focus on relevance to their day-to-day roles.

  • With executives, emphasize strategic alignment and ROI.

  • With customers, highlight benefits in plain language.

“If you say three things, you say nothing.” – Made to Stick

Business Example:
Southwest Airlines defines its brand with a simple core: “We are the low-fare airline.” Every decision—from luggage policy to route planning—is made with this audience-centered principle in mind.

2. Unexpectedness: Break the Pattern to Get Noticed

Key Insight:
To capture attention, you need to violate expectations—not with shock value alone, but with relevant surprises that trigger curiosity and open a loop the audience wants to close.

Strategies:

  • Begin with a counterintuitive or surprising statement that challenges what your audience assumes.

  • Frame data or ideas in unexpected ways to reset attention.

  • Use “curiosity gaps”—pose a question your audience wants answered.

Audience Application:

  • For resistant stakeholders, start with a bold insight that reframes their assumptions.

  • For passive audiences, generate engagement through contrast or tension.

Business Example:
When a safety campaign opened with “Did you know most workplace injuries happen just before lunch?”—it grabbed attention by challenging expectations.

Audience Tip:
Don't surprise just to entertain. Make sure the twist serves the message, not distracts from it.

3. Concreteness: Be Specific, Not Abstract

Key Insight:
People understand and remember concrete ideas better than abstract ones. Tailoring your message means finding language and imagery your audience can visualize and relate to.

Strategies:

  • Replace vague terms with sensory, real-world examples.

  • Use stories, analogies, and metaphors that make sense to the audience’s world.

  • Anchor abstract goals in day-to-day experiences.

Audience Application:

  • For senior leaders: tie your abstract strategy to clear outcomes, e.g., “increasing market share by 4% over 12 months.”

  • For employees: ground change initiatives in tasks they perform—“Instead of entering data manually, you’ll scan it using this new tool.”

Business Example:
Instead of saying “We need operational efficiency,” say, “We’re reducing wait times at checkout from 7 minutes to 3.”

Concrete language = shared understanding. Vague language = confusion or disengagement.

4. The Curse of Knowledge: The Expert’s Blind Spot

Key Insight:
Once you know something deeply, it becomes hard to imagine not knowing it. Experts often fail to communicate clearly because they assume the audience has the same background knowledge.

Strategies:

  • Step out of your expert mindset. Ask: If I were hearing this for the first time, what would confuse me?

  • Test your message with someone outside the target audience.

  • Use analogies, examples, and FAQs to bridge the knowledge gap.

Audience Application:

  • With non-technical stakeholders, translate jargon into plain language.

  • Avoid acronyms and industry terms unless you're sure of shared understanding.

Real Example:
A healthcare startup once marketed a feature as “EHR-integrated workflow enhancement” to patients. Reframed as “a tool that saves your doctor time and gets you seen faster,” it landed far better.

Reflection Questions for Strategic Communicators

Encourage students to use these before drafting any business message:

  1. What does this audience already know?

  2. What assumptions might they have that I can challenge?

  3. What specific result or action do I want from them?

  4. How can I make the message vivid or concrete?

  5. What’s one thing I can cut to make the message clearer?
