The Culture Map by Erin Meyer

Summary: Communication & Evaluating Chapters
Understanding how cultures give, receive, and interpret messages and feedback

Chapter: Communicating – High-Context vs. Low-Context

Key Insight:
Cultures vary in how explicit they are when sharing information. In low-context cultures, clarity comes from the words themselves—communication is direct, clear, and precise. In high-context cultures, meaning is embedded in tone, body language, shared understanding, and what’s not said.

Low-Context Cultures (e.g., U.S., Germany, Australia)

  • Say what they mean; transparency is valued.

  • Writing and speaking tends to be structured and literal.

  • Repetition and clarity are seen as helpful, not insulting.

High-Context Cultures (e.g., Japan, China, Saudi Arabia)

  • Expect the listener to “read between the lines.”

  • Meaning often depends on the relationship, situation, and shared history.

  • Direct speech may be viewed as rude or lacking nuance.

  • Business Application:
    If you're working with a high-context team and you're used to low-context communication, you may come across as blunt or insensitive. Conversely, if you're used to high-context cues and work with a low-context audience, you might come off as unclear or evasive.

Pro Tip:
When in doubt, mirror the communication style of the group—but default to clarity when communicating across diverse teams.

Chapter: Evaluating – Direct vs. Indirect Negative Feedback

Key Insight:
Cultures also differ in how they give and receive feedback, especially negative feedback. This dimension is often closely tied to the communication style.

Direct Negative Feedback Cultures (e.g., Netherlands, Russia, Israel)

  • Feedback is straightforward and blunt.

  • Criticism is delivered openly, often with fewer softeners.

  • Seen as honest, efficient, and constructive.

Indirect Negative Feedback Cultures (e.g., Japan, Thailand, many Latin American countries)

  • Feedback is delivered gently, often wrapped in praise or implied language.

  • Avoids embarrassment or confrontation.

  • Seen as respectful and diplomatic.

Business Application:
When giving feedback in a global context, what feels “helpful” in one culture may feel rude or disrespectful in another. Similarly, what seems “vague” may actually be a culturally appropriate way of expressing concern.

Pro Tip:
Use the “feedback sandwich” (positive–constructive–positive) cautiously. In some cultures, it builds trust; in others, it causes confusion.

Why This Matters for Global Communication

Together, these two dimensions—communication and evaluating—can cause major misunderstandings in international teams, especially when combined. For example:

  • A U.S. manager may believe they’re being “clear,” while a Japanese colleague may hear them as too harsh.

  • A Dutch team member may give honest feedback that feels offensive to an Indian colleague used to a more diplomatic tone.
