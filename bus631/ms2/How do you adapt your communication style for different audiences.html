How do you adapt your communication style for different audiences?

Powered by AI and the LinkedIn community - Retrieved from LinkedIn community advice platform 

1) Identify your audience

Before you communicate with any audience, you need to identify who they are, what they care about, and what they expect from you. This will help you determine the purpose, tone, format, and content of your communication. For example, if you are communicating with senior executives, you may want to focus on the strategic value and outcomes of your program, use a formal and concise tone, and present your information in a dashboard or a summary report. If you are communicating with technical team members, you may want to focus on the details and challenges of your program, use a casual and collaborative tone, and present your information in a meeting or a chat.

2) Choose the right channel

Once you know your audience, you need to choose the right channel to communicate with them. The channel refers to the medium or tool you use to deliver your message, such as email, phone, video conference, or face-to-face. The channel you choose should depend on the nature, urgency, and complexity of your communication, as well as the preferences and availability of your audience. For example, if you need to communicate a critical issue or a change request to your sponsor, you may want to use a phone call or a video conference to ensure clarity and immediacy. If you need to communicate a status update or a feedback request to your customers, you may want to use an email or a survey to provide convenience and documentation.

3) Use clear and simple language

Regardless of your audience and channel, you need to use clear and simple language to communicate your message. This means avoiding jargon, acronyms, slang, or technical terms that your audience may not understand or appreciate. It also means using short and simple sentences, active voice, and positive words to convey your message. For example, instead of saying "The program is experiencing some delays due to unforeseen dependencies and resource constraints", you could say "We are working hard to overcome some challenges and meet our deadlines". Using clear and simple language will help you avoid confusion, misunderstanding, or frustration among your audience.

4) Provide relevant and useful information

Another way to adapt your communication style for different audiences is to provide relevant and useful information that meets their needs and interests. This means filtering and prioritizing the information you share according to the audience's role, level, and involvement in your program. It also means providing context, evidence, and examples to support your information and make it more meaningful and actionable. For example, if you are communicating with vendors, you may want to provide specific and detailed information about the scope, requirements, and deliverables of your program, as well as examples of previous or similar projects. If you are communicating with team members, you may want to provide general and high-level information about the vision, goals, and benefits of your program, as well as evidence of your progress and achievements.

5) Listen and engage

Finally, adapting your communication style for different audiences is not only about what you say, but also about how you listen and engage. This means showing respect, empathy, and curiosity to your audience, and inviting their feedback, questions, and opinions. It also means acknowledging their concerns, addressing their issues, and offering your support and guidance. For example, if you are communicating with stakeholders, you may want to listen to their expectations, concerns, and suggestions, and engage them in the decision-making and problem-solving process. If you are communicating with team members, you may want to listen to their challenges, successes, and ideas, and engage them in the recognition and learning process.
