The Business Writer’s Handbook by Gerald J. Alred (co-authored with Charles T. Brusaw and Walter E. Oliu) is a comprehensive, practical reference guide for professional and academic business writing. It’s designed to be both instructional and a ready reference. 

Core Insights from The Business Writer’s Handbook

1. Clarity Is the Cornerstone of Effective Writing

  • Avoid ambiguity: Use precise language, especially when explaining complex ideas or giving instructions.

  • Favor short, active sentences: Passive voice and long-winded explanations often obscure the message.

  • Use plain English: Jargon and corporate buzzwords dilute clarity and alienate your audience.

  • Structure with purpose: Use paragraph breaks, headings, and bullet points to guide the reader through your ideas.

2. Tone and Audience Shape Message Effectiveness

  • Adapt your tone: The same message should sound different when written to a peer, supervisor, or client. Tone should reflect the audience’s expectations and relationship to the writer.

  • Avoid emotional or overly casual language in formal settings: This includes exclamation points, emojis, and slang unless specifically appropriate for the audience (e.g., social media posts).

  • Maintain professionalism without being robotic: A warm, respectful tone typically balances authority and approachability.

3. Message Planning Is Just as Important as Writing

Before writing:

  • Identify your purpose (to inform, persuade, request, etc.)

  • Analyze your audience (knowledge level, values, decision-making style)

  • Choose your channel (email, memo, social post, presentation)

  • Decide on your tone and structure

Use the "ABC Format" (Abstract, Body, Conclusion) to teach strategic planning:

  • Abstract – Opening summary or purpose

  • Body – Detailed explanation or supporting points

  • Conclusion – Next steps, call to action, or summary

4. Design and Layout Affect Readability

  • Use white space, headings, and lists to increase readability.

  • Embrace visual hierarchy (headings, bold, italics) to draw the eye.

  • Be mindful of typographic elements (font, size, alignment) in reports and digital communications.

5. Revising Is a Strategic, Not Cosmetic, Process

  • Rewriting involves restructuring for clarity, not just fixing grammar.

  • Look for:

    • Overused or vague words

    • Sentences that can be simplified

    • Paragraphs that lack focus or transitions

  • Edit in stages: Start with big-picture revisions, then move to details like word choice and grammar.

 The handbook encourages a "macro to micro" editing approach—ideal for your Message Dissection Lab activity.
